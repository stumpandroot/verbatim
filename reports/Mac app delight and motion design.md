# One hero object carries all the delight

The best-designed native Mac apps feel alive not because they animate a lot but because they concentrate nearly all of their motion, color, and sound into one hero object (Things' Magic Plus button, Wispr Flow's dictation pill, Not Boring's checkbox) and leave every standard control, keyboard action, and reading surface untouched. Across Apple's guidelines, the designers who write most precisely about motion, and the transcription apps reviewers praise, the numbers converge: **feedback under 300 ms, hover and press at 100–150 ms, springs with bounce 0 to 0.15 for utility work and 0.3 at most for a celebratory moment, and zero animation on anything a keyboard triggers repeatedly** ([Emil Kowalski](https://emilkowal.ski/ui/great-animations); [WWDC23 Animate with springs](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10158/); [Reshaped tokens](https://reshaped.so/docs/tokens/animation)). In the transcription and voice-memo category specifically, the praised apps have no mascots and almost no illustration; their "character" is a live audio meter that stops on release and a transcript that appears in its place, with no spinner at all ([Wispr Flow UX reference](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/@agent-native/core@0.124.6/corpus/templates/clips/desktop/design-refs/wispr-ux.md)). On macOS 26, Apple's own rule is that Liquid Glass belongs only to the floating navigation layer and that brand color belongs in the content layer underneath it, which happens to be exactly where a warm paper, iris, coral, and lime palette can live without fighting the system ([WWDC25 Meet Liquid Glass](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/219)). Performance follows one rule from Apple's own animation model: built-in modifiers such as `scaleEffect` and `opacity` animate off the main thread without touching your view code, while custom `Animatable` conformances, keyframes, and `TimelineView` plus `Canvas` re-run work every frame on the main thread ([WWDC23 Explore SwiftUI animation](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10156/)). For Verbatim, that argues for a capture dock that is the single animated hero, a Verbie who appears at state changes rather than living on screen, reading views that stay still, and an implementation that uses spring-animated built-in modifiers everywhere except the live meter, which should be driven by the render server or a worker thread rather than by SwiftUI body updates. The sources are strong on principles and on Apple's APIs, thinner on measured animation values for any specific shipping app, and silent on macOS-specific benchmarks for mascot rendering; those gaps are flagged where they matter.

## The best Mac apps animate objects, not commands

Every app in the research that reviewers call delightful follows the same allocation. Things 3 puts its personality into the Magic Plus button, which lives "in the lower right corner" and "can be dragged and dropped into different spaces of the app to do different things," and into the moment a task "pop[s] out into a card-like form while the rest of the list fades into the background"; the sidebar, by contrast, is pure typography, with "areas represented by bold text" and projects "in a lighter font," and the app is "dominated by white space" ([MacStories review of Things 3](https://www.macstories.net/reviews/things-3-beauty-and-delight-in-a-task-manager/)). Even haptics are rationed to three actions: dragging tasks, the Magic Plus button, and pull-down-to-search ([MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/reviews/things-3-beauty-and-delight-in-a-task-manager/)). Not Boring Habits, the most maximalist app in the set, layers "maybe six or seven things in the checkbox" and surrounds it with "explosive 3D animations, custom sounds, and playful haptics," yet Andy Allen is explicit that "most of [our] standard controls are fairly off-the-shelf, which makes things very simple and predictable ... We didn't have to rethink how a button or a popover works" ([Apple Developer, Behind the Design](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=9ab1g4r3)). The lesson is not restraint for its own sake; it is that one lavish object reads as personality while ten lavish objects read as noise.

Raycast proves the inverse from the other direction. Its stated principles are "fast, simple, and delightful," but Emil Kowalski cites it as an app with "no animations" that nonetheless "feels right," and he derives his most quoted rule from it: a 500 ms enter animation in a launcher "would feel slow and disconnected," so "never animate keyboard initiated actions" because users repeat them many times a day ([Raycast blog](https://raycast.com/blog/a-fresh-look-and-feel); [Emil Kowalski, Great Animations](https://emilkowal.ski/ui/great-animations)). Raycast's 2022 redesign spent its effort on scannability and confirmation instead: a larger search bar, larger leading icons "so users can scan results faster," and a bottom action bar that holds actions, navigation titles, and toasts "inside the app window" ([Raycast blog](https://raycast.com/blog/a-fresh-look-and-feel)). Apple's HIG says the same thing in its own words: "In apps, generally avoid adding motion to UI interactions that occur frequently," "aim for brevity and precision in feedback animations," and "let people cancel motion" ([Apple HIG, Motion](https://developer.apple.com/tutorials/data/design/human-interface-guidelines/motion.json)). The combined rule for a Mac utility is simple: animate objects (a card, a pill, a dock) with spring physics and sound; respond to commands (shortcuts, list navigation, paste) instantly.

The failure modes reviewers actually name are not "too much motion" but unreliable or undiscoverable motion. Rauno Freiberg's test is that an effect "is delightful if it stays dependable under extreme input and clearly improves the feel of the interface. It becomes gimmicky when it looks impressive in a demo but fails in real use," with a text cursor that glitches during fast typing as the cautionary case ([UX Tools, Your UI needs more Walt Disney](https://www.uxtools.co/blog/your-ui-needs-more-walt-disney)). Arc's critique at Pratt is about affordance, not animation: bookmarking by dragging has "no way of gaining this knowledge through the current design," and "new icons show up on random hover" is listed as a defect ([Pratt IXD, Design Critique: Arc Browser](https://ixd.prattsi.org/2023/02/design-critique-arc-browser/)). Even Fast Company's "almost overdesigned" label for Not Boring is softened by the observation that the app "seems to be having fun as it conveys information," and Allen concedes the trade: "It's never going to be a majority of people. We kind of like that" ([Brand Knew / Fast Company](https://www.brandknewmag.com/?p=20392)). A worth-stating caveat: no MacStories, Setapp, or 9to5Mac review in the research labels any 2023–2026 Mac app "too animated," so the boundary is drawn from designer essays, not from a body of user complaints.

Craft, which Tim cites as a reference, is documented only thinly. Hack Design describes the interface as one that "stays minimal until you need it," with a `+` revealed on sidebar hover and a `/` slash menu ([Hack Design, Craft](https://www.hackdesign.org/toolkit/craft/)). The specific spring values attributed to Craft (page open at response 0.35 and damping 0.85 with matched-geometry expansion; block insert at response 0.3 and damping 0.8) come from a third-party design guide that illustrates the author's own sketches, not from Craft's source, and should be treated as plausible SwiftUI defaults rather than measurements ([Blake Crosley, Craft guide](https://blakecrosley.com/guides/design/craft)). The same applies to that author's Raycast values (HUD confirmations that "fade and scale out after about 1.5 seconds," confetti "only for earned achievements," a 12pt continuous-corner window on `.ultraThinMaterial`) ([Blake Crosley, Raycast guide](https://blakecrosley.com/guides/design/raycast)). Lookaway, the other reference, could not be fetched at all; secondary coverage confirms only that it uses an "unobtrusive notification" whose position is adjustable and "subtle animations" for wellness reminders ([ifun.de](https://www.ifun.de/lookaway-mac-app-setzt-bildschirmpausen-durch-232437); [AlternativeTo](https://alternativeto.net/news/2025/2/lookaway-1-10-update-is-out-with-wellness-reminders-deep-focus-features-and-more)). Rauno Freiberg's "Invisible Details of Interaction Design," Linear's design posts, and Arc's own design writing were all unreachable, so hover-intent delays and optimistic-UI specifics are absent from this report.

## The numbers converge on 300 ms, bounce 0.15, and a 100 ms press

Where sources do give numbers, they agree closely enough to form a default set. Kowalski's rule is that "your animations should also usually be shorter than 300ms," paired with `ease-out` for anything that responds to input and a 60 fps floor below which "the other principles lose value" ([Emil Kowalski, Great Animations](https://emilkowal.ski/ui/great-animations)). web.dev puts ease-out and ease-in in the **200–500 ms** range and bounce or elastic easings at **800–1200 ms** "so the effect has time to settle," used sparingly ([web.dev, Choosing the right easing](https://web.dev/articles/choosing-the-right-easing)). Three independent production design systems publish nearly identical tiers: Reshaped uses **100 ms** for press states, **150 ms** for hover and focus, **200 ms** for overlays and toasts, and **300 ms** for long-distance travel ([Reshaped](https://reshaped.so/docs/tokens/animation)); Polar's Orbit uses **120 ms** for hover, **200 ms** as the base, **320 ms** for larger surfaces, and **480 ms** for full-screen moments ([Polar Orbit](https://orbit.polar.sh/foundations/motion)); a third set runs 50, 100, 200, 400, and 700 ms across the same categories ([davaux UI docs](https://davaux.codeberg.page/ui-docs/motion)). All three split easing the same way: decelerate for elements entering, accelerate for elements leaving, and a standard curve for elements moving while on screen, which matches the Disney "timing" rule that "enter transitions can run longer so users can take in content, while exit transitions should be quicker" ([UX Collective, Disney's 12 principles](https://uxdesign.cc/disneys-12-principles-of-animation-exemplified-in-ux-design-5cc7e3dc3f75)).

Apple replaces duration-plus-curve with duration-plus-bounce, and the WWDC23 session is the one place in the research that gives perceptual anchors. Bounce **0** is "a great general purpose spring that's the most versatile"; around **0.15** "doesn't feel very bouncy yet, but the long tail feels a little more brisk"; around **0.3** "you do start to feel some noticeable bounciness"; and "be cautious about using values higher than around 0.4, since they may feel too exaggerated for a UI element" ([WWDC23 Animate with springs](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10158/)). Apple adds bounce "if you want your animation to feel a little more playful" or "when you want an animation to feel more physical, like if it's going to be used at the end of a gesture," and asks you to pick one register for the whole app ([WWDC23](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10158/)). Springs are the default for a bare `withAnimation` because "springs are the only type of animation that maintains continuity both for static cases and cases with an initial velocity," so an interrupted spring "uses the velocity it had when it was retargeted," which is the property that makes hover-out and drag-release feel physical ([WWDC23](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10158/)). Linear curves get a specific warning: fine "for a repeating spinning indicator, but otherwise you should be cautious about using them, especially for movement" ([WWDC23](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10158/)). The presets `.smooth`, `.snappy`, and `.bouncy` exist with tuning forms like `.snappy(duration: 0.4)` and `.snappy(extraBounce: 0.1)` ([WWDC Notes](https://wwdcnotes.com/documentation/wwdc23-10158-animate-with-springs/)), but the session never states their numeric values; the commonly cited 0.5 s duration with bounce 0, 0.15, and 0.3 respectively is prior knowledge, not verified here.

Two further numbers help with lists and secondary elements. A stagger example uses **100 ms per item** with each item animating over 400 ms, and the first item must start at 0 ms because "a delay in the initiation of the effect can make a user think that something went wrong" ([LogRocket, CSS staggered animations](https://blog.logrocket.com/css-staggered-animations/)). Freiberg's reading of Disney's follow-through puts inner elements (icons, labels) "100–200ms after the main transition" when a button morphs into a field or a modal opens ([UX Tools](https://www.uxtools.co/blog/your-ui-needs-more-walt-disney)). Two values Tim will want are not in any source: hover scale and press scale. Common practice is roughly `scale(1.02)` on hover and `scale(0.97–0.98)` on press over about 100 ms with a spring release, and Apple's 2025 note that Liquid Glass "produces a more subdued effect when a person interacts using a trackpad" argues for the small end of that range on Mac ([Apple HIG, Motion](https://developer.apple.com/tutorials/data/design/human-interface-guidelines/motion.json)); treat the scale figures as inference. Reduced motion is settled: Kowalski swaps a 0.2 s bounce for a 0.2 s fade rather than deleting feedback, and Apple's Reduce Motion "decreases the intensity of some effects and disables the material's elastic properties" on glass ([Emil Kowalski](https://emilkowal.ski/ui/great-animations); [Meet Liquid Glass transcript](https://nonstrict.eu/wwdcindex/wwdc2025/219/)).

| Pattern | Value the sources support | Source quality |
|---|---|---|
| Hover in/out | 100–150 ms, `ease` or ease-out, color or opacity shift; scale ≤1.02 | Durations cited; scale inferred |
| Press | ~100 ms down, spring release bounce ~0.15; scale 0.97–0.98 | Duration cited; scale inferred |
| Small state change (chip, toggle, selection) | 200 ms, or `.spring(duration: 0.3, bounce: 0)` | Cited |
| Sheet, popover, panel entering | 250–320 ms decelerate, transform origin at the trigger | Cited |
| Same element exiting | 20–40% shorter, accelerate | Cited (ratio inferred) |
| List or chip stagger | 100 ms per item for ≤5 items; 30–60 ms for longer lists | 100 ms cited; shorter step inferred |
| Secondary element follow-through | 100–200 ms after the primary | Cited |
| Celebratory spring (done, success) | bounce 0.2–0.3, never above 0.4 | Cited |
| Reduced motion variant | Same duration, opacity only, no translation or bounce | Cited |
| Keyboard-triggered action | No animation | Cited |

## Transcription apps make the meter the character and skip the spinner

The transcription and dictation apps that reviewers praise share a surprising amount of visual restraint, and the praise is explicitly for that restraint. Wispr Flow is "a dark, floating panel over whatever app you're using" whose "UI asks almost nothing of the user," with "a subtle waveform animation" while listening and no separate editing stage ([Abduzeedo](https://abduzeedo.com/node/89253)). Its state model is the clearest pattern in the research: while the hotkey is held, a bottom-center bar expands and shows white vertical bars rising and falling with input volume; on release the bars stop; "there is no separate spinner or 'thinking' state"; the pasted text is the success signal; a start chime plays at record start; and audio under about 0.5 seconds is discarded ([Wispr Flow UX reference, third-party compilation](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/@agent-native/core@0.124.6/corpus/templates/clips/desktop/design-refs/wispr-ux.md)). Granola's pill does the same with green bars that "freeze when paused," detaches to the screen edge with a drag handle when you switch apps, and hides when idle; its co-founder describes the product goal as "a calm product experience for people with crazy workdays" and says the team is "almost paranoid" about adding anything to the core meeting view ([Granola UX reference, third-party](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/@agent-native/core@0.124.6/corpus/templates/clips/desktop/design-refs/granola-ux.md); [Cognitive Revolution podcast](https://www.cognitiverevolution.ai/calm-ai-for-crazy-days-inside-granola-s-design-philosophy-with-co-founder-sam-stephenson/)). The apps that get criticized are the dense ones: Fireflies is "overwhelming," tl;dv "cluttered," and Superwhisper's recording overlay is "intrusive for some users" with onboarding that leaves "new users ... feeling overwhelmed" ([Asian Efficiency](https://www.asianefficiency.com/technology/best-ai-meeting-note-takers/); [Spokenly comparison, competitor-authored](https://spokenly.app/blog/wispr-flow-vs-superwhisper-vs-macwhisper)). The two pill sizes and pixel estimates in the third-party references (Granola's collapsed pill at roughly 120–160 by 32–40 px, expanding to 360–420 px) are screenshot-derived, and no source gives animation timings or easing for any pill's idle-to-listening transition.

Apple's own apps set the bar for the recording and transcript states a third-party app must match. On Mac, Voice Memos' Transcription button toggles the same region between waveform and text during and after recording, "the current word highlighted" live, with "selecting a term in the transcription mov[ing] the playhead to that point" and library search that matches "title *or* transcription" ([Apple Support, Voice Memos on Mac](https://support.apple.com/guide/voice-memos/vm4a03609f0d)). Notes on Mac embeds the recording in the note body so you "keep editing the note while recording," lets you click any line to seek, and offers Find in Transcript, Copy Transcript, Add Transcript to Note, and Summary ([Apple Support, Notes on Mac](https://support.apple.com/guide/notes/apdb5106e334)). The iOS 26 recorder is a big red button, a live waveform, yellow trim handles, and turtle and rabbit icons on the speed slider; that is the entire extent of Apple's "fun" in the category ([iGeeksBlog, Voice Memos in iOS 26](https://www.igeeksblog.com/how-to-use-voice-memos-on-iphone-ipad/)). Where Apple leaves room is speaker labels, in-place transcript editing, exports, and local-model choice, which MacWhisper 14 now fills with an Editor View that lets you "edit your transcript directly in the main transcript screen," "reassign speakers faster," and favorite segments ([9to5Mac, MacWhisper 14](https://9to5mac.com/2026/07/14/macwhisper-14-launches-with-a-new-transcript-editor-faster-performance-more/)). Descript's keyboard model is the correction benchmark: press `e` to pause and correct, with playback resuming after the edit, hold `w` to cycle punctuation with automatic recapitalization, and Cmd+R to insert a speaker break ([Tuts+, Descript corrections](https://photography.tutsplus.com/tutorials/how-to-correct-ai-transcriptions-descript--cms-36948)).

Progress and processing states follow the same quiet model. Granola's AI pane "stays empty during the meeting and fills only after 'Enhance notes'," loading is "quiet with no modal or heavy spinner," and errors show "a simple retry"; the delight stories are about absence of waiting, with notes appearing in Slack 30 seconds after a meeting ([Granola UX reference](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/@agent-native/core@0.124.6/corpus/templates/clips/desktop/design-refs/granola-ux.md); [Cognitive Revolution](https://www.cognitiverevolution.ai/calm-ai-for-crazy-days-inside-granola-s-design-philosophy-with-co-founder-sam-stephenson/)). MacWhisper starts transcription on file drop "with no confirmation step or setup screen" ([Dave Swift, MacWhisper review](https://daveswift.com/macwhisper/)). Empty and first-launch states are documented only sparsely: Bears Gratitude, the 2024 Delight and Fun award winner, has "no sign-in screen, so users can start writing right away" ([The Note, Bears Gratitude](https://thenote.app/post/en/the-good-news-bears-inside-the-adorably-unorthodox-design-of-bears-gratitude-k89vshmfmf)), and Wispr Flow's onboarding spends its personality on a "Try It Yourself" sandbox with simulated Gmail and Notion fields rather than on illustration ([Wispr Flow UX reference](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/@agent-native/core@0.124.6/corpus/templates/clips/desktop/design-refs/wispr-ux.md)). One operational warning from Superwhisper is worth copying as a feature: during system-audio capture its waveform "stays still by design," which confuses users enough that the vendor documents it, so a distinct "capturing app audio" indicator is better than a flat meter ([Superwhisper docs, Recording window](https://superwhisper.com/docs/get-started/interface-rec-window.md)). No source in the research describes an empty-state illustration, app icon, or onboarding artwork for any transcription app, and no reviewer praises a mascot-driven one; that absence is the finding.

## Mascots animate on state, not on a clock

The best-documented character systems are state-driven. Duolingo builds its characters in Rive, "chosen for compact file sizes, a clean fit with the app's architecture, and a smooth handoff from animator to engineer," with a state machine that is "a visual representation of the logic that connects the animations"; each character has 20-plus mouth shapes, body poses and mouth states run as separate simultaneous states, characters stop speaking if the user finishes early and move to a reacting state for correct or incorrect answers, and the idle set is limited to "head nods, blinking, and eyebrow movement" ([Duolingo blog, World character visemes](https://blog.duolingo.com/world-character-visemes)). Duo's app icon changes expression when the app has not been opened for a long time, which is the same principle applied to absence ([Diseñando Apps](https://disenandoapps.substack.com/p/27-detalles-invisibles)). Bears Gratitude's creators say "the art is the heart of the app," and Apple's 2025 citations reward "charming, consistent illustrations," a flash-card transition "accompanied by a real-world sound," and haptics on unlocking gems ([The Note](https://thenote.app/post/en/the-good-news-bears-inside-the-adorably-unorthodox-design-of-bears-gratitude-k89vshmfmf); [Apple Design Awards 2025](https://developer.apple.com/design/awards/2025/)). A Rive tutorial that mirrors Duolingo's rig lists the standard inputs (`isSpeaking`, `isThinking`, `isCorrect`, `isWrong`) and the standard idle layers (blink controller, breathing control, pupil-tracking controller), with "blink + talk + breathe" running concurrently ([dev.to, Duolingo-style mascot in Rive](https://dev.to/uianimation/building-a-duolingo-style-interactive-mascot-in-rive-step-by-step-guide-2d5c)).

Numbers for idle behavior are thin. The only cited interval is a game-engine convention that schedules each blink **5–10 seconds** after the previous one, with a secondary idle roughly every fourth blink and the two never overlapping ([AGS forums](https://www.adventuregamestudio.co.uk/forums/beginners-technical-questions/solved-randomized-idle-blinking)). No Duolingo, Headspace, or Apple source gives blink rate, breathing amplitude, or eye-tracking range, and no source quantifies how often a mascot can react per session before it annoys. Apple's constraints still apply to a character that sits on screen all day: avoid motion on frequent interactions, let people cancel motion, and (from visionOS, but physiological) avoid sustained oscillation near 0.2 Hz "because people can be very sensitive to this frequency," keeping amplitude low ([Apple HIG, Motion](https://developer.apple.com/tutorials/data/design/human-interface-guidelines/motion.json)). Design Lobster's note that fidget-style delight "carries weak evidence for its claimed benefits" and can become a distraction is the honest counterweight ([Design Lobster, Fidgetability](https://designlobster.substack.com/p/150-fidgetability)). On Mac specifically, no surveyed app documents a mascot system; Not Boring's "character" is the checkbox itself, Wispr Flow's is the pill, and the transferable idea is that the hero object can carry the personality the character would otherwise carry.

## Liquid Glass claims the chrome and leaves the content layer to you

On macOS 26 every standard toolbar, sidebar, sheet, menu, and control picks up Liquid Glass with no code once the app is built with the Xcode 26 SDK, and every custom glass API (`glassEffect`, `Glass`, `GlassEffectContainer`, `glassEffectID`, `glassEffectUnion`, `.glass` and `.glassProminent` button styles, `ToolbarSpacer`, `backgroundExtensionEffect`, `scrollEdgeEffectStyle`) is macOS 26.0 and later, so an app that also ships on macOS 14 and 15 needs an `if #available(macOS 26, *)` branch for each custom use and nothing at all for standard chrome ([Apple, Adopting Liquid Glass](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/technologyoverviews/adopting-liquid-glass); [Apple, GlassEffectContainer](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/glasseffectcontainer); [Apple, Glass](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/glass)). Apple's design rule is unambiguous and, for a warm, colorful app, convenient: "Liquid Glass belongs to the navigation and control layer. Keep it out of the content layer, and use standard materials for content-layer elements such as app backgrounds," with transient sliders and toggles the one exception ([Apple HIG Materials, mirrored](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/@tmaasen/apple-dev-mcp/blob/b82f0efe2115dc4539c83a2374a714a84aeb350a/content/universal/materials.md)). "If you want to imbue color into your app, do it in the content layer instead," "tinting should only be used to bring emphasis to primary elements and actions," and "always avoid glass on glass" ([WWDC25 Meet Liquid Glass](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/219)). The sidebar glass samples what is beneath it ("light from colorful content nearby can subtly spill onto its surface"), and the Mac-specific behaviors are that large glass elements "don't flip from light to dark," controls "nest perfectly into the rounded corners of windows," and "when a window loses focus on the Mac ... Liquid Glass shifts its appearance and visually recedes" ([WWDC25 219](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/219)).

The concrete layout recipe comes from Apple's Landmarks sample and its adoption guide. Group toolbar items by function with `ToolbarSpacer(.fixed)` (Apple's incorrect example is Undo, Redo, Markup, and More in one pill; the correct one is Undo plus Redo in one pill and Markup plus More in another), "don't mix text and icons across items that share a background," and use `.confirmationAction` placement or a tint for exactly one primary action ([Apple, Adopting Liquid Glass](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/technologyoverviews/adopting-liquid-glass); [Swift with Majid, Glassifying toolbars](https://swiftwithmajid.com/2025/07/01/glassifying-toolbars-in-swiftui/)). "Reduce your use of custom backgrounds in controls and navigation elements" because they "interfere with Liquid Glass or other effects that the system provides, such as the scroll edge effect," and run content under the sidebar with `backgroundExtensionEffect` so the sidebar does not "float above an empty background" ([Apple, Adopting Liquid Glass](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/technologyoverviews/adopting-liquid-glass)). A plain tinted color works with that effect (`ZStack { tint.opacity(0.2).backgroundExtensionEffect(); content }`), and glass "look[s] less glassy on plain colors," so test against the real paper surface ([troz.net, SwiftUI for Mac 2025](https://troz.net/post/2025/swiftui-mac-2025/)). The hidden-title-bar case has a documented fix: on macOS 26 `.windowStyle(.hiddenTitleBar)` left "a blank strip at the top of the window," solved with `.containerBackground(.clear, for: .window)` (macOS 15 and later) and `.toolbar(removing: .title)` on the sidebar ([fatbobman, Zipic retrospective](https://fatbobman.com/en/posts/zipic-3-technical-details/)). Custom glass motion has its own rule: coordinate morphing inside one `GlassEffectContainer` with `glassEffectID` and `@Namespace`, use `.matchedGeometry` within the container's spacing and `.materialize` beyond it, and "limit the use of Liquid Glass effects onscreen at the same time" because "creating too many Liquid Glass effect containers ... can degrade performance" ([Apple, Applying Liquid Glass to custom views](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/applying-liquid-glass-to-custom-views)). One developer measured why: each glass view renders through a `CABackdropLayer` needing extra offscreen passes, and wrapping two glass views in one container halved the backdrop count, though that test was on iOS ([JuniperPhoton](https://juniperphoton.substack.com/p/adopting-liquid-glass-experiences)).

Three practical constraints shape what to build. First, the design cannot depend on glass looking a particular way: macOS 26.1 added a user-selectable Clear or Tinted appearance, and Reduce Transparency "disables all Liquid Glass effects," so the chrome may be an opaque gray bar on some Macs ([Eclectic Light Co.](https://eclecticlight.co/2025/11/05/appearance-revisited-get-tahoe-26-1-looking-in-better-shape/)). Second, opting out is a bridge, not a plan: `UIDesignRequiresCompatibility` is labeled "temporarily use this key" and "the system ignores this key when you build for ... macOS 27 or later" ([Apple, UIDesignRequiresCompatibility](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/bundleresources/information-property-list/uidesignrequirescompatibility)). Third, the known 26.x bugs cluster where custom views meet the toolbar: SwiftUI buttons overlaid on the title-bar area "don't receive clicks" because an `NSGlassContainerView` swallows hit testing (workaround `.buttonStyle(.borderless)`, filed FB18201935, no Apple response), and a title-bar accessory can "visually cover the first row of a sidebar" under Reduce Transparency ([Apple Developer Forums 788928](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/788928); [Apple Developer Forums 827686](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/827686)). The commentary critique is also worth hearing before putting controls over content: "controls are not tools," and glass controls "bleed into your document, thus gaining a similar level of importance as the document itself" ([Michael Tsai, Content vs. Controls](https://mjtsai.com/blog/2025/10/16/liquid-glass-content-vs-controls/)). For a transcription app whose reading surface is the product, that is an argument for a calm, opaque-feeling content area under a thin glass toolbar rather than floating glass controls over the transcript. Third-party adopters (Fantastical, Bear, MindNode, Flighty, Portal) are confirmed only by name; no source analyzes a colorful third-party app's glass adoption in detail ([BGR](https://bgr.com/1972706/best-mac-apps-updated-macos-tahoe/)).

## SwiftUI animates built-in modifiers for free and charges per frame for everything else

Apple's own description of its animation system is the cost model. A state change invalidates a view and "at the close of the transaction, the framework calls body to produce a new value," but the frames of an animation on a built-in attribute never touch your code: "For built-in animatable attributes like scaleEffect, SwiftUI is very efficient. It's able to do this work off the main thread and without calling into any of your view code" ([WWDC23 Explore SwiftUI animation](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10156/)). The expensive path is anything that forces per-frame evaluation: "a custom Animatable conformance can be much more expensive to animate than a built-in effect because it'll run body for every frame of the animation," and keyframes "provide you with a value of this type on every frame," "so you should avoid performing any expensive operations while applying a keyframe animation," and "can't gracefully retarget the way that springs can" ([WWDC23 10156](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10156/); [WWDC23 Wind your way through advanced animations](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10157/)). `phaseAnimator` is Apple's own choice for an ambient loop ([false, true] on a 1 s `easeInOut`), and `TimelineView(.animation(minimumInterval:paused:))` is documented as updating "no more quickly than the provided interval," which is the throttle for anything date-driven ([WWDC23 10157](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10157/); [Apple, AnimationTimelineSchedule](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/animationtimelineschedule)). Two specific anti-patterns are sourced: `repeatForever` re-issued from a timer stacks animations until CPU climbs toward 95 percent ([Apple Developer Forums 767243](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/767243), anecdotal), and "it's important to avoid storing values that update really often, such as geometry values or timers, in the environment" because "every view that reads the environment depends on the whole EnvironmentValues struct" ([WWDC25 Optimize SwiftUI performance with Instruments](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/306/)). Transactions have a scoping rule too: prefer `.animation(_:value:)`, which "will only write the animation into the transaction if the value has changed," over blanket `.transaction` overrides that "can lead to accidental animations" ([WWDC23 10156](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10156/)).

Dependency hygiene decides whether a hover or a meter invalidates one view or the whole window. `@Observable` re-evaluates a view only when a property it read in `body` changes, whereas `ObservableObject` invalidates every dependent view on any `@Published` change; Apple's WWDC25 case cut a whole-list re-render to "only two updates, one for each button" by moving per-item state into per-item `@Observable` view models stored in an `@ObservationIgnored` dictionary ([fatbobman, Observation deep dive](https://fatbobman.com/en/posts/mastering-observation/); [WWDC25 306](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/306/)). For pointer-following highlights and tilt, `onContinuousHover(coordinateSpace:perform:)` (macOS 13 and later) delivers `.active(location)` and `.ended`, and a published tilt implementation maps normalized offset to `rotation3DEffect` clamped at ±10° with a spring reset ([mjtsai.com summarizing Panferova](https://mjtsai.com/blog/2023/02/06/tracking-hover-location-in-swiftui/); [zenn.dev tilt card](https://zenn.dev/touyou/articles/flip_tilt_card_swiftui)). Because each hover callback writes `@State`, the hovered card's body runs on every pointer event, so the card must be a tiny leaf view; attaching `.animation(.spring(duration: 0.3), value: hoverPoint)` lets SwiftUI interpolate between samples off-thread, and "SwiftUI will now automatically track velocities any time a gesture is changing properties," so the release spring inherits pointer velocity ([WWDC23 10158](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10158/)). No Apple source recommends a hover throttle; ignoring sub-point deltas or routing through a `TimelineView` capped at display rate is a reasonable engineering choice, not a sourced one. One macOS-specific reliability bug matters for a paper-colored window: `.onHover` fails sporadically on views that do not cover the window when the window background is clear, with a non-clear background as the workaround ([mjtsai.com](https://mjtsai.com/blog/2023/02/06/tracking-hover-location-in-swiftui/)).

For continuously moving things (a live meter, a breathing mascot) the research supports leaving SwiftUI's body path. Core Animation layer animations run in the render server; Apple's own engineers advise driving an `NSViewRepresentable` layer from the SwiftUI transaction with `NSAnimationContext.animate(context.transaction.animation) { ... }` or adopting `Animatable`, and note that `.onChange`-triggered layer updates on macOS can snap instead of animating until bridged that way ([Apple Developer Forums 832885](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/832885)). Rive's Apple runtime supports macOS 13.1 and later under an MIT license, wraps in SwiftUI through `RiveUIViewRepresentable`, runs each `Worker` as roughly one background thread with GPU rendering, lets you pin `.fps(30)` or a range, and offers editor listeners for Pointer Enter, Exit, Move, Down, and Up ([Rive Apple runtime](https://rive.app/docs/runtimes/apple/apple); [Rive listeners](https://rive.app/docs/editor/state-machine/listeners)). Lottie's default engine is "Core Animation with Main Thread fallback," and the docs do not list which features trigger the fallback ([lottie-ios configuration](https://tessl.io/registry/tessl/cocoapods-lottie-ios/4.5.0/files/docs/configuration.md)). Two gaps are important enough to state plainly: no published benchmark compares `TimelineView` plus `Canvas`, `CALayer`, Rive, and Lottie on macOS, and no source confirms that rive-ios delivers hover-without-click mouse movement to Pointer Move listeners on Mac; the second must be verified before committing Verbie's eyes to Rive. `drawingGroup()` is also not a free win: it rasterizes a subtree through Metal and fixed a 100-gradient example, but "adding the off-screen render pass might slow down SwiftUI for simple drawing" ([Hacking with Swift](https://hackingwithswift.com/books/ios-swiftui/enabling-high-performance-metal-rendering-with-drawinggroup)).

Tahoe adds a few known hazards and one better profiler. Reports (none confirmed by Apple) include "even simple animations like a .frame change of a Circle won't animate smoothly" in an `NSHostingView` inside a status item on macOS 26, Marcin Krzyzanowski's "SwiftUI layout is broken on macOS 26, in entirely new ways," and lingering capsule artifacts when a toolbar `Menu` plus `Picker` dismisses after building with Xcode 26 ([Apple Developer Forums 803501](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/803501); [mjtsai.com Tahoe RC roundup](https://mjtsai.com/blog/2025/09/10/macos-tahoe-26-rc/); [Apple Developer Forums 820846](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/820846)). The research did not find any documented regression in which SwiftUI transactions rebuild the scene, toolbar, or menus on macOS 26, so if Verbatim is seeing that, it is undocumented rather than a known platform bug. On the positive side, WWDC25 says Lists with over 100,000 items on macOS "now load 6x faster" and "update up to 16x faster," and Xcode 26's SwiftUI Instruments template adds Update Groups, Long View Body Updates, Long Representable Updates, and a Cause & Effect Graph, colored by "how likely they are to contribute to a hitch or hang," with the instruction to "investigate red updates first" ([WWDC25 What's new in SwiftUI](https://nonstrict.eu/wwdcindex/wwdc2025/256/); [WWDC25 306](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/306/)). Apple publishes no millisecond budget per body; the only remark is that "a millisecond to run a view body may not seem like a long time, but the total time spent can really add up," so the acceptance test is structural: during an idle loop, only the animating leaf should appear in Update Groups, and the Hangs and Hitches lane should be empty ([WWDC25 306](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/306/)).

## What Verbatim should animate, with what values, built how

Verbatim's brief ("fun, colorful, animated, interactive, and minimal," Apple-native, with the Listening Desk capture dock as the signature object and Verbie reserved for moments) lines up with the research better than a first read of "minimal" might suggest. The evidence says the way to get all five adjectives at once is to make the capture dock the one hero object that gets physics, color, and sound; keep reading views (transcript, notes, library list) almost entirely still; let Verbie appear on state changes and at empty states rather than idle on screen; and build the whole thing on spring-animated built-in modifiers so it stays smooth while a Whisper model is running. The table below is the prioritized plan. Priority 1 items are what make the app feel alive and should ship first; priority 2 items are the second pass; priority 3 items are the ones to build only after profiling shows headroom. Three design choices in that table deserve explanation. First, the dock is the only object that gets bounce above 0.15, color beyond a single tint, and sound, because every source from Things to Wispr Flow to Not Boring shows that one lavish object reads as personality while scattered motion reads as noise, and Apple's HIG adds the frequency rule on top. Second, Verbie appears at state changes because Duolingo's system and the Rive rig pattern are state-driven, and because no transcription app that reviewers praise uses a mascot at all; a Verbie who shows up when the library is empty, when a transcript finishes, or when something fails will feel like a helper, while a Verbie who idles beside the transcript will compete with the one surface that must stay calm. Third, the palette goes on cards, backgrounds, chips, and the dock rather than on chrome, because Apple's content-versus-navigation rule means paper, iris, coral, and lime will read through a glass sidebar on macOS 26, will sit on standard materials on 14 and 15, and will still look right when Reduce Transparency turns the chrome into an opaque bar.

Where the sources are thin, say so in the design review. No shipping Mac app's actual spring values or durations were measured; the Craft and Raycast numbers in circulation are a third-party author's illustrations. Hover scale, press scale, Mac tilt angle, stagger steps under 100 ms, Verbie's breathing amplitude and period, and the 30 fps meter cap are my recommendations inside sourced ranges, not citations. Rauno Freiberg's and Linear's writing, Lookaway's own site, and Apple's HIG Liquid Glass page were unreachable, so hover-intent delays and Lookaway's overlay design are absent. No macOS benchmark compares Canvas, Core Animation, Rive, and Lottie, and Rive's macOS hover delivery is unverified. No source gives a mascot reaction frequency or a per-body millisecond budget. The macOS 26 transaction-rebuild regression the brief hinted at is not documented anywhere the research reached.

## Conclusion

The practical insight from this research is that "alive" on the Mac is a property of allocation rather than quantity: the apps people love concentrate motion, color, and sound into one object users touch on purpose and leave everything else as fast and native as a Finder window. That changes how Verbatim's brief should be read. "Fun, colorful, animated, interactive" describes the capture dock and Verbie's moments; "minimal" and "Apple-native" describe the transcript, the library, the toolbar, and every keyboard path. Liquid Glass makes this split literal on macOS 26 by claiming the chrome and handing the content layer to the brand, and SwiftUI's animation model makes it cheap, because built-in modifiers driven by springs cost nothing per frame while the one continuously moving thing (the meter) can live in Core Animation.

The follow-up work is narrow and testable. Measure rather than guess the two values the sources could not supply by prototyping the dock and the hover card in code and reviewing them a day later, as Kowalski suggests; verify Rive's macOS hover behavior before promising Verbie tracking eyes; and run the SwiftUI Instruments template on the recording screen with a model loaded, treating an empty Hangs and Hitches lane and a single leaf in Update Groups as the definition of done. If those three checks pass, the design holds on macOS 14 through 26 without a compatibility key, and the app will feel like it belongs on the Mac while still being unmistakably Verbatim.
